From BBM92 Measurements to Secure Keys: Challenges in a Practical Implementation

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics November 18, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article spotlights the often‑overlooked gap between textbook BBM92 security proofs and the messy realities of laboratory implementations, showing that even modest hardware choices—such as halving detector count with a time‑multiplexed receiver—can introduce loopholes that jeopardize provable secrecy. By rigorously characterising passive polarisation analysers and exposing post‑processing hurdles with a CW‑pumped SPDC source, the work connects directly to the current push for scalable, cost‑effective QKD systems while reminding developers that every simplification must be reconciled with composable security frameworks. The findings serve as a timely cautionary benchmark for both academic groups and commercial vendors aiming to move entanglement‑based QKD from proof‑of‑concept toward robust real‑world deployment.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

The protocol introduced by C. H. Bennett, G. Brassard and N. D. Mermin in 1992 (BBM92) is one of the most important and widely adopted quantum key distribution (QKD) protocols. As with any other QKD protocol, its practical security depends on satisfying the assumptions underlying its theoretical security proofs. Here, we examine two common implementations of BBM92: a conventional passive receiver and a time-multiplexed configuration that halves the number of detectors. We characterise the passive polarisation analysers common to both architectures and present experimental results obtained with the time-multiplexed receiver, employing a continuous-wave (CW) pumped spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC) source of polarisation-entangled photon pairs and threshold detectors. We investigate the challenges encountered during post-processing when attempting to satisfy the requirements of existing security proofs and discuss the gap between theoretical security assumptions and the extraction of a provably secure cryptographic key.
