Reading long-range information from a separable quantum record requires entangled measurements

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics November 10, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article demonstrates that a completely separable array of qubit probes can nonetheless encode long‑range spin correlations, but extracting that information reliably demands joint (entangled) measurements rather than any locally implementable strategy. By pinpointing the precise overlap window where separable readouts collapse to random guessing while entangled readouts retain a distance‑independent advantage, it clarifies an operational separation between state entanglement and measurement entanglement—a theme echoed in recent advances on distributed quantum sensing and resource theories of measurements. The result suggests that future quantum sensor networks may need collective readout hardware to unlock the full informational content of locally generated records, even when the probes themselves remain unentangled.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

Qubit probes record the relative orientation of neighboring, initially uncorrelated Ising spins through local interactions. Each probe encodes one bond label in two states with overlap $1-p$. The resulting probe record is fully separable. A receiver gets one copy and guesses whether two distant spins point in the same or opposite directions. On the square lattice, the best readout with separable measurement effects approaches success probability $1/2$ as the spin separation grows, for $0<p\le p_0=1-1/\sqrt2$. This includes all local operations with classical communication. The same conclusion holds for every measurement whose effects remain positive under partial transposition across every cut of the probes. For $p_*=0.29181\le p\le1$, a joint readout of the same record maintains a success probability above $1/2$ by a positive amount independent of distance. The overlap $[p_*,p_0]$ gives a strict separation between the readouts. A record that is created locally and contains no entanglement can therefore hold long-range information that only measurements with entangled effects can read.
