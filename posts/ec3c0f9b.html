Efficient simulation of variational quantum circuits: representation, accuracy and CPU/GPU allocation

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics November 18, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article tackles a bottleneck in quantum‑software development by systematically benchmarking how different classical simulation strategies—statevector, Pauli‑term evolution and matrix‑product‑states—perform on modern CPU/GPU hardware across a range of variational ansätze. By showing that direct Pauli updates can outpace traditional statevector methods for chemically relevant circuits while GPUs deliver up to thirty‑fold speedups for larger spin models, the work gives practitioners concrete guidance on when to trade precision for throughput and how to allocate resources under memory or core limits. The resulting decision framework helps bridge the gap between algorithm design and realistic performance expectations, a crucial step as industry moves toward hybrid quantum‑classical workflows and seeks reliable baselines for claiming quantum advantage.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

Classical circuit simulation supports quantum software development and verification and provides a baseline for assessing quantum advantage. We compare gate-based statevector simulation, direct Pauli evolution and matrix product states for molecular and spin Variational Hamiltonian Ansatze and hardware-efficient ansatze on CPU/GPU systems, varying width, depth, precision and resource allocation. In the tested implementations, direct Pauli evolution benefits complex molecular VHA, whereas GPUs accelerate wider spin and hardware efficient circuits. Matrix product state is useful when limited entanglement permits accurate compression; corrected CPU FP32 can meet the tested tolerance while costing more than FP64. Workload size, startup, worker memory and task-specific profiling can change the preferred allocation. Matched NumPy Pauli updates accelerate two-layer full LiH by 3.45-fold; the GPU gives approximately 30-fold acceleration over 18-thread Aer for 28-qubit, four-layer TFIM, both in FP64. We provide a procedure for choosing representation, precision and worker allocation jointly, comparing the cost of complete energy evaluations under accuracy, memory and core constraints.
