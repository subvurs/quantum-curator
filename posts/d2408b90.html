The speed limit of gravitational entanglement and the cost of noise

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics November 16, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article pinpoints the fundamental rate at which two massive objects can become entangled solely through their mutual gravity, showing that—even in an ideal noiseless world—the speed drops as the inverse cube of their separation and that realistic environmental noise pushes the scaling down to an even steeper inverse‑fourth law. By deriving exact limits for continuous error‑correction schemes, it clarifies how much decoherence must be suppressed before a tabletop test can decisively rule out classical‑gravity explanations, linking the proposal directly to ongoing efforts by groups such as those at UChicago and Vienna. The results give experimentalists concrete benchmarks for mass size, distance, and coherence time, while also tempering expectations with the stark trade‑off that protecting against noise simultaneously hides the very gravitational signal they seek.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

Is gravity quantum? A proposed test lets two small masses, each in a superposition of two places, become entangled through their mutual gravity. Gravity is weak, so the masses must stay coherent for seconds while random forces disturb them. Here we ask how fast gravity can entangle two masses, and what noise costs. Without noise we find the maximum rate exactly: it falls as the inverse cube of the separation, and two positions per mass achieve it. Above a noise level set by the masses and their separation, gravity's leading pull can be imitated by classical signals, and every strategy is limited to a rate falling at least as the inverse fourth power. Exact, continuous error correction shields the masses from noise but hides them from each other: the best rate falls as the inverse seventh power, and six positions on a hexagon come within 10% of it at large separation.
