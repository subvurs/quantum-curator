Quantum Parallel Algorithm for Multivariate Trace Estimation with Applications

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics November 18, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article introduces a near‑term‑friendly quantum algorithm that computes multivariate traces with circuit depth independent of the number k of matrices, simply by trading extra qubits for parallelism across up to O(k) devices. By framing trace estimation as a tensor‑network problem, the authors connect to recent low‑depth Hamiltonian‑simulation and distributed‑quantum‑computing efforts, showing that spectral‑sum and Lanczos‑type calculations can be accelerated without costly polynomial constructions. The result could make a range of scientific simulations—such as partition‑function or density‑of‑states estimates—more practical on noisy intermediate‑scale quantum hardware, provided enough qubits are available to host the parallel registers.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

Estimating multivariate traces Tr(A1A2......Ak) is a fundamental task arising across diverse fields. In this paper, We develop a quantum algorithm for this problem tailored to near-term quantum devices. We access matrices by preparing their vectorized states. This access mode yields a tensor-network representation of the multivariate trace, which in turn guides the design of a parallel algorithm. The resulting circuit has depth dominated solely by that of the input oracle and independent of the number of matrices k, at the cost of a qubit count scaling as Θ(k)-a trade-off between circuit depth and circuit size. Moreover, the circuit exhibits a simple modular structure that allows explicit distribution over O(k) devices. We further establish efficient preparation circuits for a broad range of matrix classes, demonstrating the wide applicability of our approach. we present two applications: spectral sum estimation and a quantum implementation of the classical Lanczos method. Both reduce the target problem to sums of multivariate traces, thereby avoiding the construction of matrix polynomial transformations and achieving two levels of parallelism. For certain matrix classes, the resulting algorithms reduce circuit depth by a factor equal to the degree of the approximating polynomial. The shallow circuit depth and distributed implementation make our algorithms particularly suitable for near-term devices.
