A Concise Introduction to Quantum Electromagnetics

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics November 13, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article matters because it bridges the gap between classical electromagnetics and quantum optics, giving engineers a concrete toolkit for deciding when full‑quantum models are required in sensing applications such as atomic clocks or Rydberg‑based receivers. By showing how familiar mode profiles, Green’s functions and scattering matrices can be directly imported into quantum descriptions, it accelerates the design of low‑noise, bandwidth‑aware sensors that claim a genuine quantum advantage. The work also clarifies the limits of those advantages—highlighting the role of loss, noise and measurement resources—so readers can realistically assess performance gains against classical baselines. In short, it equips the community with practical modeling guidance at a time when quantum‑enhanced metrology is moving from laboratory demos toward commercial deployment.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

This book introduces quantum electromagnetics to readers trained in classical electromagnetics, without assuming a systematic background in quantum mechanics. The central question is when classical descriptions suffice, and when the quantum states of matter or the field must be retained explicitly. Starting from familiar electromagnetic modes and material response, the discussion develops atomic dynamics, field quantization, and the interaction of quantized fields with quantum matter. Particular attention is given to how mode profiles, Green functions, and scattering matrices obtained from classical electromagnetic calculations enter quantum models. Atomic coherence, photon statistics, spontaneous emission, and cavity coupling are developed through derivations that identify the physical assumptions and limits of each description. Examples drawn from atomic clocks, Rydberg-atom receivers, and single-photon devices connect these concepts to excitation, propagation, and measurement in practical systems. Loss and noise are included in relating internal dynamics to measured signals and correlations. The final discussion examines how sensitivity and claims of quantum advantage depend on bandwidth, measurement resources, and the classical reference used for comparison. The aim is to help readers choose an appropriate model, carry out the calculation, and understand what its predictions mean for an electromagnetic measurement.
