A provably efficient classical algorithm for simulating OTOC$^{(1)}$ in 1D

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics November 18, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article delivers the first rigorously proven polynomial‑time classical algorithm for estimating first‑order out‑of‑time‑order correlators in one‑dimensional Haar‑random brickwork circuits, closing a long‑standing gap between empirical evidence of tractability and formal guarantees. By introducing “window propagation,” the authors show that even deep random circuits—up to hundreds of qubits—can be simulated with inverse‑polynomial error, which tempers expectations that OTOC measurement alone will furnish near‑term quantum advantage. The result also provides a concrete benchmark for future hardness proofs and highlights that higher‑order OTOCs in two dimensions remain the more promising frontier for demonstrating genuine quantum supremacy.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

Out-of-time-order correlators (OTOCs) are quantities of broad interest in many-body physics due to their connection with information scrambling and quantum chaos. Recently, estimating higher-order OTOCs in random quantum circuits was proposed as a promising avenue for quantum advantage, yet the classical hardness of this task remains poorly understood. Even for first-order OTOCs, where growing evidence suggests classical tractability, no efficient classical algorithm with rigorous guarantees has been established to date. In this work, we introduce "window propagation," the first provably polynomial-time classical algorithm for approximating first-order OTOCs in 1D Haar-random brickwork circuits, at arbitrary depths and with inverse-polynomial mean-squared error. We also conduct numerical experiments comparing our simulation results to exact computation on up to $512$ qubits for Clifford circuits with $768$ $T$-gates and up to $22$ qubits for Haar-random circuits. We emphasize that our results do not directly apply to second-order OTOCs in 2D, which is the subject of recent quantum advantage proposals. Nevertheless, our work provides a rigorous step towards understanding the classical complexity of OTOC estimation and represents a family of non-trivial quantum circuits for which a provable, polynomial time algorithm is now known, establishing foundations for understanding these widely used diagnostics of quantum chaos.
