Native higher-order optimization with entropy quantum computing

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics November 25, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article shows that entropy‑based quantum computing can natively encode cubic and quartic objective functions—something that forces annealers into costly quadratization and gate‑model processors into deep circuit compilations. By solving six industrial‑scale instances on the Dirac‑3S photonic‑electronic platform, including a portfolio problem with 80 000 terms, the authors demonstrate feasible solutions within 0.32 % of the best known values while avoiding embeddings that would demand >10⁵ physical qubits or millions of two‑qubit gates on other hardware. The result opens a practical pathway for tackling higher‑order optimization problems on near‑term quantum devices, though further work is needed to assess scalability and error robustness as problem sizes grow.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

Higher-order interactions are common in applied optimization: parity checks, radar-code sidelobes, portfolio skewness and kurtosis, and marketing-channel synergies all yield cubic or quartic objectives. Quantum annealers and most Ising machines accept only quadratic objectives, and gate-based quantum processors build every higher-order term from two-qubit gates. Entropy quantum computing, realized in the hybrid photonic-electronic Dirac-3S system, accepts polynomials of degree up to five with all-to-all coupling over binary, integer or simplex-constrained continuous variables. No user-supplied quadratization or circuit compilation is needed. We formulate four industrial use cases as six cubic and quartic instances, run them on Dirac-3S with 30 samples each, and score every sample against a certified optimum or a classical best-known value. We also estimate their cost on quantum annealers, superconducting grids and trapped-ion registers under standard mappings. For the two densest instances, a portfolio with about 80,000 terms and a budget allocation with 360,000 terms, the quadratized problems need embeddings far beyond current annealers, over $10^5$ physical qubits for the portfolio alone, and one phase-separation layer costs over $10^6$ two-qubit gates before optimization. Dirac-3S ran all six natively and returned feasible, distinct solutions. All samples of the binary instances are single-flip local minima. The best portfolio sample lies within 0.32% of the best-known value and the best budget sample 15% above it, but no sample reached its reference. Classical solvers reach the references within minutes at these sizes, and random starts followed by a local polish reach comparable or better values, so the advantage established here is one of expressibility and mapping cost, not of solution quality. Whether it extends to time to solution at larger sizes remains open.
