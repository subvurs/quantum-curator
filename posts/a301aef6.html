Teleportation of Logical Qubits using Lattice Surgery with Superconducting Circuits

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics November 17, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article reports the first experimental teleportation of logical qubits using lattice‑surgery on a superconducting processor with 41 physical qubits, demonstrating that surface‑code patches can be merged and split while preserving encoded information. By showing how repeated stabilizer measurements restore fault tolerance and improve fidelity, it connects directly to recent efforts by Google, IBM and academic groups to build scalable error‑corrected architectures. The ability to move logical states without physically shuttling qubits paves the way for modular quantum processors, although the distance‑three code still leaves room for higher fidelities before full fault‑tolerant operation is achieved.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

Demonstrating a quantum advantage when solving large-scale computational problems on a quantum computer requires the correction of errors that occur due to limitations in control accuracy and coherence. Using the concept of quantum error correction, fault-tolerant logical qubits can be realized and manipulated with logical single- and two-qubit gates. In this paper, we experimentally analyze fault-tolerant lattice-surgery, which is a key primitive that enables operations between logical qubits realized in the surface-code. Using this primitive, we demonstrate the teleportation of logical states between two distance-three logical qubits realized in a superconducting circuit composed of 41 physical qubits. Throughout this process, we study the step-by-step transfer of logical information from a source to a target logical qubit by characterizing the time evolution of logical observables. We find that repeated execution of stabilizer measurements, after merging two surface-code patches, restores fault tolerance and yields increased fidelity of the teleported state. When optimizing the protocol in terms of gate count and sequence duration, we observe further improvements in fidelity. Our work offers new insights into the optimization of fault-tolerant lattice-surgery operations, while also providing a comprehensive description of the physical device and gate sequences used. We expect the efficient implementation of increasingly complex logical circuits to enhance the performance of future fault-tolerant quantum computers.
