When lies look like noise: certifying untrusted entanglement swapping

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics November 19, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article tackles a subtle but critical security gap in entanglement‑swapping repeaters by showing that dishonest nodes can hide misreported measurement outcomes behind ordinary readout noise, making standard verification ineffective. By introducing a cut‑and‑choose certification scheme that tests only a random subset while preserving the remaining pairs for use, it offers a realistic quality‑of‑service guarantee compatible with emerging quantum‑network protocols. The analysis also highlights today’s memory bottleneck—certifying even a modest 1 % misreport rate on a million links would require storing up to a million entangled pairs—underscoring the need for scalable quantum memories before such safeguards can be deployed at scale.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

Entanglement swapping lets quantum repeaters distribute entanglement over long distances, a prerequisite for the quantum internet. We study its security in a noisy network whose intermediate nodes perform the quantum operations faithfully but may misreport the classical outcome that end users rely on. We show that the ideal functionality of entanglement swapping cannot be realised, because it gives the adversary no influence over the delivered state, whereas a node that relabels its report does have such influence. Including honest noise in the ideal setting does not help unless it also concedes a bounded fraction of misreported pairs. We therefore propose a cut-and-choose protocol that tests a random subset of pairs to certify the rest. Since no test can distinguish a misreport from readout noise, the protocol certifies the error above a pre-agreed minimum noise level, which is the misreport rate under our threat model. This yields a quality-of-service guarantee, in which a failure witnesses that the node did not deliver as agreed. Unlike verification schemes that consume the state they certify, the untested pairs remain available for use, with a finite-size guarantee. Certifying a 1% misreport rate on 10^6 pairs costs 10^4 to 1.7x10^5 test pairs, for minimum noise levels ranging from 0 to 5%. The main cost is memory, since 1% requires holding 10^5 to 10^6 pairs at once, far beyond the capacity of today's quantum memories. Verifying and releasing in blocks ties the memory to the block size at the cost of a weaker rate: a (10^4)-qubit memory certifies 14% on 4.7x10^5 delivered pairs.
