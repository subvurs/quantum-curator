Bounding Fidelity of Non-Stabilizer States from Local Pauli Measurements

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics October 30, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article tackles a long‑standing bottleneck in quantum verification by showing how the “magic” needed for universal computation can be certified with only linear‑scaling Pauli measurements, sidestepping the exponential blow‑up that plagues direct fidelity estimation of non‑stabilizer states. By exploiting permutational symmetry and casting the problem as a small linear program, the authors demonstrate practical protocols that remain tractable even for hundreds of qubits—a scale relevant to near‑term quantum processors and distributed quantum networks. The approach not only cuts measurement overhead but also opens the door to efficiently monitoring other nonlinear resources such as purity, although its performance still depends on how well the assumed symmetries match real hardware noise.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

Nonstabilizerness, commonly referred to as magic, is essential for universal quantum computation. Yet, for pure $n$-qubit states this property is directly linked to the hardness of direct fidelity estimation. We propose a scheme to bound the fidelity on non-stabilizer states by choosing operators with low measurement overhead and using parallel local Pauli measurements to estimate compatible observables. We selectively measure operators that share a permutational symmetry without imposing any assumption on the physical system. This selection reduces the optimization to a linear program with only $O(n)$ variables for bounding fidelity. Our methods can be extended to bound nonlinear quantities like the purity via convex quadratic programs. For hypergraph states that can exhibit extensive nonstabilizerness, we demonstrate for up to $n=500$ qubits that our framework substantially reduces the measurement overhead under both local and global depolarizing noise. These results show that symmetry induced by measurement selection reduces both measurement and computational costs, enabling scalable characterization of magic resources in quantum computing and quantum networks.
