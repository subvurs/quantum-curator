Hybrid Quantum Algorithms for Trade Surveillance Analytics

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics October 30, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article shows that hybrid quantum‑classical classifiers can already rival most conventional machine‑learning models on a realistic anti‑money‑laundering task, with the HQNN beating six of eight classical baselines and delivering competitive F1 scores even on noisy IBM hardware. By combining a tiny five‑qubit circuit with a classical network that sets every rotation angle, the authors demonstrate a practical route to leverage near‑term quantum devices for high‑dimensional financial analytics—a field where recent advances in quantum kernels and QNNs have struggled to scale. Although XGBoost still leads, the results suggest that hybrid approaches could soon narrow the gap between quantum prototypes and production‑grade fraud detection systems.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

Anti-money laundering (AML) and trade surveillance remain largely manual: rule-based monitoring raises alerts of which, by estimate, over 95% are false positives, each reviewed by a human analyst. We benchmark four quantum and hybrid classifiers against classical baselines on a synthetic pump-and-dump detection task: a fidelity-based quantum k-nearest-neighbours classifier (QKNN) with clustering-based routing; a quantum support vector machine (QSVM) on a one-body projected quantum kernel; a 20-qubit quantum neural network (QNN) with a Pauli-ZZ feature map and RealAmplitudes ansatz; and a hybrid quantum neural network (HQNN) in which a small classical network generates every rotation angle of a five-qubit circuit. Baselines are logistic regression, XGBoost, one- and three-hidden-layer neural networks, and classical k-nearest neighbours. All models share one stratified split of 1000 accounts (800 train, 200 test, 20% suspicious) and two 20-feature representations (ANOVA and PCA); QKNN and QSVM are also tested under simulated noise, and the HQNN is run on the ibm_fez processor. The best model is classical XGBoost on ANOVA features, at a suspicious-class F1 of 61.4%. The HQNN is the strongest quantum-hybrid model, at 54.5-57.9% F1 on an ideal simulator and 55.4-59.8% on ibm_fez, ahead of six of eight classical configurations; at a 15% false-positive rate, half its alerts are genuinely suspicious. Versus the QNN (42.0% F1), it raises under half the false alarms at similar recall with a quarter of the qubits. The QKNN reaches 43.5% F1 (44.4% under noise), above classical KNN (35.6%); the QSVM does not exceed 41%. We observe no quantum advantage, but a five-qubit hybrid on today's noisy hardware already matches well-tuned classical models, with room to grow via richer circuits and larger devices. We contribute a like-for-like comparison with classical models on shared data, splits and features.
