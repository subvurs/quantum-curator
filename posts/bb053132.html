Quantum phase estimation on a Rydberg quantum simulator

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics November 13, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article marks the first demonstration of quantum phase estimation on an analog Rydberg‑atom simulator, allowing researchers to extract absolute ground‑state energies and full excitation spectra without auxiliary qubits or controlled gates. By reconstructing Loschmidt echoes and Fourier‑transforming them, the team achieves phase‑sensitive many‑body spectroscopy across up to 19 atoms in one‑ and two‑dimensional lattices—a capability that goes well beyond traditional gap measurements and brings analog platforms closer to digital quantum‑algorithm performance. The result opens a practical route for probing complex material and chemical models on near‑term hardware, though scaling beyond the current atom count will still require advances in coherence and control precision.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

Determining the energy spectrum of a many-body quantum system is a central goal of quantum physics. Ground-state energies underpin materials science and quantum chemistry, whereas excited states govern dynamical phenomena such as quantum thermalization. Quantum simulators have greatly shaped our understanding of many-body systems, for example, by enabling access to microscopic mechanisms and nonlocal order parameters. However, experiments still struggle to access the full range of many-body spectral features and are typically limited to measuring energy gaps and excitations by spectroscopic means. Here, we address this challenge by demonstrating quantum phase estimation on an analog Rydberg quantum simulator. We reconstruct the complex-valued return amplitude, or Loschmidt echo, of an initial probe state. From its Fourier transform, we measure absolute ground-state energies and energy gaps in systems of up to 19 atoms in one and two spatial dimensions. Varying the initial probe state, we select distinct spectral windows within which we compute the longitudinal and transverse magnetization, and observe their competition across the many-body spectrum. Our results thereby demonstrate phase-sensitive many-body spectroscopy on an analog quantum simulator without auxiliary qubits or controlled evolution.
