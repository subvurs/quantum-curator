Classical sampling of verifiable graph-state quantum advantage experiments

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics November 24, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article shows that the current “verifiable quantum‑advantage” proposals can still be outpaced by modest classical hardware, underscoring how delicate the balance between verifiability and hardness truly is. By exploiting the built‑in error‑detection structure, the authors classically sampled the 70‑qubit graph‑state experiment with higher fidelity than IBM’s own processor, revealing that T‑gate placement—forced to hide from the verifier—leaves a tractable tail for classical simulators. The work therefore maps a concrete obstacle for future advantage demonstrations and signals that any next‑generation verifiable benchmark must deliberately scramble or conceal non‑Clifford resources to avoid such classical shortcuts.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

Quantum computers are expected to outperform classical ones on some tasks, but a convincing demonstration must be both hard to reproduce classically and verifiable. A recent proposal achieves verifiability by preparing graph states with built-in error detection, so that the quality of the experiment can be certified without simulating it; hardness is added by measuring in a non-Clifford basis or by inserting T gates. We show that the two instances posted to demonstrate this proposal on IBM hardware can be sampled classically on eight graphics processing units, with higher quality than the hardware in comparable or shorter time. For the 70-qubit instance our samples are drawn from the ideal output distribution; for the 64-qubit instance with 314 T gates they come with a certified fidelity of at least 0.908, against 0.349 for the hardware. The reason lies in verification itself: T gates can only be placed where the error detection cannot see them, which pushes them towards the end of the circuit, where they add little classical cost. Our results identify the features that future verifiable advantage experiments must control.
