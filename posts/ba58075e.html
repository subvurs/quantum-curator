Fermion Scattering on Quantum Computers

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics October 23, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article marks one of the first concrete demonstrations that a hybrid qubit‑qumode processor can simulate real‑time fermion–antifermion scattering and resolve resonance formation in a (1+1)‑dimensional Yukawa model, providing a practical benchmark for near‑term quantum hardware. By combining digital encoding of fermions with continuous‑variable representation of the scalar field, the authors bridge the gap between abstract quantum‑field‑theory algorithms and experimentally accessible photonic platforms, complementing recent tensor‑network studies of low‑dimensional QFT dynamics. The ability to extract decay rates and Breit–Wigner resonance profiles with percent‑level accuracy shows that local observables on hybrid devices can already probe nontrivial particle physics phenomena, though scaling to higher dimensions and larger lattices will still demand significant advances in error mitigation and hardware connectivity.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

Resonant scattering links particle interactions to the formation and decay of unstable excitations. We develop a hybrid quantum-computing framework to study these dynamics through fermion--antifermion collisions. Our formulation of $(1+1)$-dimensional Yukawa theory encodes fermions in qubits and the scalar field in qumodes. We identify the operations needed for a photonic implementation and benchmark the dynamics with tensor-network simulations. Near resonance, we resolve the build-up of a localised scalar excitation and the subsequent partial return of energy to the fermions. Enhanced entanglement accompanies this exchange. Time-dependent correlator measurements reproduce the analytically predicted decay rate to a few percent precision, with energy scans exhibiting a resonance peak consistent with packet-averaged Breit--Wigner behaviour. These results establish a concrete scattering benchmark for hybrid quantum computers and demonstrate how local real-time observables can probe particle-resonance dynamics.
