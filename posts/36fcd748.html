Quantifying superposition via coherence by stochastic orthogonalization

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics November 23, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article provides the first operational bridge between the abstract notion of quantum superposition and the well‑studied resource theory of coherence, showing that stochastic orthogonalization measurements can convert any superposition into a quantifiable amount of coherence. By defining a “degree of superposition” that inherits the operational meaning of existing coherence measures, it offers a practical tool for benchmarking how much useful interference a hardware platform or sensor can actually generate. The work also clarifies fundamental limits—such as why deterministic conversion to maximally coherent states is only possible for up to three reference states—informing both theoretical resource‑theory research and the design of devices that rely on precise superposition control.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

Here we present an approach to quantifying superposition of nonorthogonal reference states through quantum coherence. No change of basis achieves this: every orthonormal basis makes some superposition-free states appear coherent. We show that orthogonalization succeeds instead as a measurement mapping every reference state onto a multiple of a basis vector in each outcome; such measurements are exactly the free operations that convert superposition into coherence. The largest average coherence they extract from a state, which we call its degree of superposition, turns every coherence measure into a superposition measure with the same operational meaning. An interference term becomes coherence at most with the probability of unambiguously discriminating its two reference states, a bound attained when there are no others and, for positive coefficients and nonnegative overlaps, exactly when the overlaps are probabilities of a random grouping. Maximal degree means conversion into a maximally coherent state with certainty, possible for up to three reference states but not in general. Each coherence measure gives its own degree; when two degrees order two superpositions oppositely, neither converts into the other with certainty, and we find such pairs already for two reference states, unlike in qubit coherence. For equal positive overlaps, in-phase superpositions are rigid: deterministic conversions that keep all components can only permute their coefficients. As the reference states merge, the degree stays bounded where widely used measures can diverge, and at every amplitude a rotation-symmetric cat state attains its maximum. Our results quantify superposition operationally and reveal conversion constraints created by nonorthogonality.
