Direct-decision quantum sensing for classifying transient NMR signals

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics November 06, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article demonstrates how a task‑oriented quantum sensor can bypass full signal reconstruction and output a binary decision directly, offering a clear advantage over conventional coil readout when the inductive link is weak or noisy. By interleaving trainable control pulses with the qubit’s evolution, the authors show that ensemble‑averaged accuracy can rise sharply for certain NMR‑like waveforms—mirroring recent progress in variational quantum classifiers applied to sensing problems. The work also highlights practical limits: single‑shot discrimination remains gradual and requires either long interrogation times or unusually strong sensor coupling, underscoring the need for improved qubit–sample interfaces before such protocols become a routine tool for small‑volume molecular detection.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

Task-oriented quantum sensing aims to extract decision-relevant information directly, without first reconstructing the full signal. We study this approach for mixture-threshold classification of transient NMR-like signals generated from molecular spin Hamiltonians, motivated by small-volume molecular sensing. Using analytical models and numerical simulations, we compare classical coil readout followed by matched-filter classification with quantum computational sensing (QCS), in which trainable controls interleaved with signal-driven sensor-qubit evolution map the transient directly to a decision score. We consider both the sensor-ensemble limit and strict single-sensor single-shot readout. Increasing QCS control depth can improve robustness and class-dependent output contrast. Ensemble accuracy can rise rapidly for some waveform pairs, whereas single-shot correctness improves more gradually because one projective outcome must distinguish the classes. Repeated acquisitions improve both routes by averaging independent fluctuations, while disorder-aware training mitigates quasistatic inhomogeneity in the modeled sensor ensemble. QCS can outperform inductive readout under the modeled conditions, for example when coil transfer is weak or receiver noise limits coil performance. However, the simulated QCS protocols at lower effective sensor coupling require long sensing intervals, whereas short-time protocols require a much stronger effective drive at the sensor. Practical advantage therefore depends on jointly attainable source-signal strength, signal-to-sensor transfer, qubit coherence, and readout resources.
