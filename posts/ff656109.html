Benchmarking superconducting quantum processors with percolating entanglement quality

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics November 13, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article introduces percolating entanglement quality (PEQ), a topology‑aware benchmark that goes beyond simply counting how many qubits are linked together and instead quantifies the strength of each two‑qubit bond across an entire chip. By applying PEQ to a 64‑qubit square lattice and re‑evaluating earlier heavy‑hex results, the authors show that processors with identical cluster sizes can have markedly different entanglement fidelity, giving hardware teams a single, comparable score for disparate architectures. The metric promises to guide both error‑mitigation strategies and future coupling‑layout designs as the field pushes toward truly scalable quantum processors.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

The capability to create large entanglement resources is a key performance attribute of quantum processors, and the size of the resource is often used as a benchmark of processor scale. Recent graph-state experiments have reported entanglement clusters spanning entire chips with hundreds of qubits. However, cluster size alone does not capture the entanglement quality of individual couplings or differences in coupling topology. Here, we introduce percolating entanglement quality (PEQ), a topology-normalized benchmark based on conditional two-qubit negativity and a topology-specific bond-percolation threshold. As an experimental demonstration, we prepare a native graph state on a 64-qubit square-lattice superconducting processor with defects, identify a 52-qubit entanglement cluster, and evaluate the PEQ of the processor. Applying PEQ to previously reported chip-wide graph-state measurements on heavy-hex-lattice processors, we show that PEQ differs substantially among processors whose entanglement clusters span the entire chip. PEQ provides a single chip-wide score without selecting a best-performing qubit subset or path. This score allows processors with different coupling topologies to be compared on a common scale and can inform the design of coupling topologies for scalable chips.
