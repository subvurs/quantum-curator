Distributed Trotterization with optimal time-scaling entanglement cost

Source: arXiv Quantum Physics August 26, 2026 Curated by Mark Eatherly

Curator's Take

AI Commentary

This article shows that the long‑standing bottleneck of fixed‑per‑gate entanglement consumption in distributed quantum simulators can be eliminated by making the cost proportional to the actual interaction strength instead of the number of Trotter steps. By embedding a repeat‑until‑success primitive into product‑formula algorithms, the authors achieve an entanglement budget that grows only linearly with simulation time and is provably optimal, opening the door for high‑accuracy many‑body dynamics on modestly sized networked processors. The result bridges a gap between theoretical communication‑complexity limits and practical hardware constraints, suggesting that near‑term quantum‑network experiments could now scale to problems previously thought out of reach.

— Mark Eatherly

Summary

Distributed architectures extend quantum simulation of many-body dynamics beyond the reach of any single processor, with shared entanglement mediating interactions between spatially separated devices. Conventional implementations rely on quantum teleportation, which provides a universal realization of nonlocal operations but incurs a fixed entanglement cost per gate, irrespective of its strength. This becomes increasingly inefficient in product formula simulation, where higher accuracy requires ever more numerous, yet progressively weaker, nonlocal rotations, causing the entanglement cost to diverge in the high-accuracy limit. Here we introduce a simple repeat-until-success protocol that makes entanglement consumption adaptive to interaction strength. Incorporating this primitive into distributed product formulas yields a total entanglement cost that scales linearly with evolution time and remains independent of Trotter error. A matching lower bound from quantum communication complexity proves this time scaling to be optimal, establishing a resource-efficient foundation for high-accuracy quantum simulation across networked processors.
